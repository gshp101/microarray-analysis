What the pipeline does

A gene expression microarray measures, in one tissue sample, how much messenger RNA there is for each of roughly 20,000 genes. Compare many samples from two groups (here tumor and non-tumor liver) and you can see which genes are switched up or down in the disease. Metabolism is run by enzymes, and enzymes are made from genes, so when many genes of one metabolic pathway move together, that pathway is likely running differently.

The tool turns that idea into a fixed sequence of steps. The first eight answer the core question, which metabolic pathways are affected. Steps 9 to 12 go one stage further and ask which existing drugs might push those changes back, then check how well those drug rankings hold up.

1

The input: a GEO series matrix

Public microarray studies are deposited in NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Each study has an accession number (here GSE14520, from Roessler et al. 2010, Cancer Research) and a series matrix file: a gzipped text file holding the already-normalized measurements and a description of every sample.

The tool downloads that file from ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo. GSE14520 was run on two kinds of chip, so there are two matrix files; the tool uses the main cohort on platform GPL3921, 445 samples. The file has two parts.

Is it already on a log scale?

Raw spot intensities run from tens to tens of thousands, and their noise grows with their size. Taking log2 makes the noise roughly even and turns ratios into differences: a log2 difference of 1 means twice as much RNA. The tool uses the same rule as GEO's own GEO2R tool: if the 99th percentile of all values is above 100, or the range is wider than 50 with a positive lower quartile, the values are linear and get log2-transformed.

2

From probes to genes

Pathway databases list genes by their official symbol (CYP1A2), not by probe ID (207608_x_at). The tool downloads the platform's annotation file from GEO (GPL3921.annot.gz), which says which gene each probe measures, then cleans up:

  • 840 probes have no gene symbol (controls, unannotated sequence) and are dropped.
  • 724 probes match several genes at once (A /// B); they can't be assigned to one, so they are dropped too.
  • 4,726 genes are measured by more than one probe. For each, the tool keeps the single probe with the highest average signal, since a brighter probe is usually the one that binds its gene best.

Result: a table of 12,788 genes × 445 samples, saved as data/GSE14520/expression.tsv.gz.

3

Labeling the samples

GEO has no standard field for "case" or "control", so the tool assigns groups with two search patterns (regular expressions) run over each sample's text. For this study they are stored with the curated dataset:

tumor     →  liver tumor tissue
nontumor  →  non-tumor tissue

A sample that matches exactly one pattern gets that label; one that matches none or both is left out. The tool then compares the counts with the published study and warns on any mismatch.

Both counts match the paper. The last number matters for how the comparison should be done, see the note in the next step.

For any other GEO study you give the two patterns yourself: report GSE12345 --group tumor='tumou?r' --group normal='normal'. For your own data, a sample sheet with a group column does the same job.

4

Testing each gene

For each of the 12,788 genes the tool asks: is the average level in tumors different from the average in non-tumor tissue, by more than the sample-to-sample spread would produce by chance? Two numbers come out of that.

  • log2 fold change: tumor mean minus non-tumor mean, on the log2 scale. −1 means half as much, +2 means four times as much.
  • Welch's t statistic and its p-value: the difference divided by its standard error, allowing the two groups to have different spreads.

Correcting for 12,788 tests

With thousands of tests, a p-value cutoff of 0.05 would let through hundreds of false hits. The tool converts p-values to a false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg): FDR < 0.05 means that among all genes called changed, about 5% are expected to be false. A gene counts as clearly changed when FDR < 0.05 and it moved at least two-fold (|log2 FC| ≥ 1).

Known limitation. The t-test treats all 445 samples as independent people. In fact 213 patients gave both a tumor and a non-tumor sample. A paired test (or a mixed model) would compare each tumor with its own patient's normal tissue and remove person-to-person differences, which usually gives more power. That is not implemented yet. With this many samples the main findings are unlikely to change, but for small paired studies it matters more.
5

Collecting metabolic pathways

A pathway, for this tool, is a named list of genes. Two curated databases supply them:

  • KEGG (rest.kegg.jp): human pathways in the Metabolism category, such as "Glycolysis / Gluconeogenesis" or "Retinol metabolism". The huge overview maps ("Metabolic pathways", 1,500+ genes) are skipped because they would swamp the specific ones.
  • Reactome (reactome.org): every pathway under the top-level "Metabolism" entry.

Reactome sets made mostly of general protein machinery (ribosome, proteasome, nuclear pore subunits) are dropped, because they follow cell growth rather than metabolism. That leaves 404 sets. Of these, 222 have between 10 and 500 genes that were actually measured on this chip and are tested: 76 from KEGG and 146 from Reactome. The two databases overlap, so related pathways often appear in pairs.

With --scope all, signaling pathways (MAPK, PI3K-Akt, VEGF and so on) are scored as well.

6

Over-representation analysis (ORA)

The first pathway test is a counting question. Take the genes that went clearly down. Are more of them in a pathway than you'd expect if they had been picked at random? The same is then asked for genes that went clearly up.

The background is every measured gene that belongs to at least one tested pathway. The hypergeometric test gives the chance of seeing at least that many hits by luck.

Across all 222 pathways, 49 pathway–direction pairs reach FDR < 0.05 (pathways_ora.tsv). ORA is easy to read but has a weakness: it only sees genes past the cutoff. A pathway where 40 genes each move a little stays invisible, and metabolic pathways often change exactly that way.

7

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA uses every gene, no cutoff. All 12,788 genes are lined up from most increased (largest t) to most decreased (most negative t). Then the tool walks down the list keeping a running score: it steps up each time it meets a gene of the pathway (by an amount proportional to that gene's |t|) and steps down a little for every other gene.

If the pathway's genes are scattered randomly, the score wanders near zero. If they bunch at one end, the score peaks there. The peak is the enrichment score. It is then compared with 1,000 shuffles of the gene labels and scaled by pathway size, giving the normalized enrichment score (NES); positive NES means the pathway went up in tumors, negative means down.

The genes that sit before the peak are the leading edge: the ones actually driving the result. The drug steps later work from these.

What it means biologicallyThe tumors have lost much of normal liver chemistry: detoxifying drugs and toxins (cytochrome P450s), handling vitamin A (retinol), breaking down amino acids and fatty acids. They have turned up the machinery a growing cell needs: glucose burning (glycolysis), mitochondrial energy production, DNA building blocks (nucleotide interconversion) and cholesterol for new membranes. This matches what is known about liver cancer: cells lose their specialized liver identity and shift toward growth.

The report

Everything above is written to results/GSE14520/, and gathered into one self-contained report.html you can open offline or email. It contains:

  • a plain-language summary of which pathways moved and in which direction;

  • the volcano plot, the methods used and their caveats;

From pathways to drug leads

The tool then asks: which existing drugs might push these changes back? It tries two independent routes. Both produce research leads, not treatment advice.

Route A: drugs that act on the changed enzymes

First it collects the pathway-driving genes: leading-edge genes of pathways with FDR < 0.05 that are themselves significantly changed in the same direction. For this study that is 496 genes. It then looks each up in KEGG DRUG. A drug opposes the change if it inhibits a gene that went up, or activates one that went down.

The top of the list is reasonable: RRM2 makes DNA building blocks for dividing cells, and the drugs that block it are established chemotherapy agents. Since most enzyme drugs are inhibitors, this route works best when pathways go up (drugs_targets.tsv).

Route B: signature reversal (LINCS L1000)

The LINCS L1000 project treated cultured human cells with about 5,400 compounds and recorded which genes each one raised and lowered. For each compound the tool takes the average tumor-vs-normal t of the genes the compound raises, minus the average of the genes it lowers, and turns that into a z-score. A strongly negative z means the compound tends to lower what the tumor raised and raise what it lowered.

Genes move in co-regulated groups, so these z-scores are a ranking, not calibrated p-values. Most L1000 profiles come from cancer cell lines, which suits tumor data better than, say, brain tissue (drugs_reversal.tsv).

10

Network proximity

Many drugs act not on the changed enzymes themselves but on proteins that interact with them. The tool loads a map of about 141,000 known interactions between 13,400 human proteins (Menche et al. 2015) and treats the 496 pathway-driving genes as the disease's neighborhood in that map.

For each of 4,753 KEGG drugs it measures how many steps, on average, separate the drug's targets from the nearest disease gene. It repeats this 1,000 times with random target sets matched for how connected they are, and reports a z-score. z < −2 means closer than chance: 295 drugs here.

Read with careThe top hit is cobicistat (z = −4.8), because both its targets, CYP3A4 and CYP3A7, are disease genes. But cobicistat inhibits CYP3A4, which already went down in tumors, so it would push the same way, not back. Proximity says how close a drug is, not which direction it pushes. The table carries opposes_change and the L1000 score beside each drug for that reason (drugs_proximity.tsv).
11

Cell line dependencies (DepMap)

For cancer data, one more question: which pathway-driving genes do cancer cells of this tissue actually need to grow? DepMap knocked out genes one at a time in about 1,100 cancer cell lines with CRISPR. The tool compares the share of 22 liver cancer lines that depend on each gene with the share of all other lines (one-sided Fisher test), and flags genes that went up in tumors and that at least 1 in 10 liver lines need.

12

Checking the drug rankings against real trials

Do the rankings point at drugs that work? The tool ships a list of liver cancer drugs with known phase III outcomes: 9 with positive trials (sorafenib, lenvatinib, regorafenib and others) and 20 that failed. The roc command asks how often each route ranks a positive drug above a failed one. That is the AUC: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.

On liver cancer, none of the routes beats chance. Six of the nine positive drugs block growth-signaling proteins (kinases or the VEGF receptor), and those signaling pathways don't change much at the mRNA level (VEGF, MAPK and PI3K pathways: GSEA FDR 0.37 to 0.84 even with --scope all). Pooled over all six curated studies (liver, lung, breast, colorectal and two Alzheimer's sets, each against its own trial list), proximity does modestly better than chance.

TakeawayThe pathway findings in steps 4 to 7 are solid and agree with the biology. The drug lists are a starting point for reading, not a prediction of what will work in patients.
13

Limits and next steps

  • Independent samples assumed. The paired tumor/normal design (213 patients here; also the breast and colorectal sets) and repeated brain regions per donor (Alzheimer's sets) call for a paired or mixed model. Not implemented yet.
  • Plain Welch t-test. A moderated test (as in limma) borrows strength across genes and behaves better with small groups.
  • Gene correlation. Genes in a pathway are correlated, which makes ORA and the L1000 z-scores overconfident. A correlation-aware set test (such as CAMERA) would help.
  • Tissue make-up. Tumor vs adjacent tissue also differs in cell types (immune cells, fat, connective tissue), and those differences show up as pathway changes too.
  • mRNA is not activity. Enzyme activity also depends on protein levels, modifications and substrate supply.

Run it yourself

git clone https://github.com/gshp101/microarray-analysis
cd microarray-analysis
pip install -e .[dev]

microarray-pathways list                     # the six curated studies
microarray-pathways report liver_cancer      # steps 1-11, writes results/GSE14520/

# any GEO study: two group patterns, case group first
microarray-pathways report GSE15852 --group tumor='tumou?r|cancer' --group normal='normal'

# your own data: expression table + sample sheet with a "group" column
microarray-pathways report --expression expr.tsv --samples samples.tsv \
    --case AD --control control --name my_study

# step 12
microarray-pathways roc GSE14520 --results results_drugs --results results_proximity

The liver study downloads about 21 MB from GEO and runs in a few minutes; add --no-drugs to stop after step 8. KEGG data is free for academic use; commercial use needs a KEGG license.
